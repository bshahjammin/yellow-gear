Start Here: What is FLL Challenge?

BIOGLOW is the 2026-2027 FIRST LEGO League Challenge season. The theme is biodiversity: how living things, habitats, people, science, and technology are connected. The team will solve robot missions and also research a real biodiversity problem, design a solution, explain its engineering process, and demonstrate FIRST Core Values.

Attachment
A robot mechanism built to help complete one or more missions.
Looks like: a removable arm, fork, lift, or pusher.
Precision Tokens

Looks like: six small discs beside the field.

Testing, learning, changing something, and testing again.
Looks like: build → test → improve → repeat.

Doing great work while respecting and helping others.
Looks like: excellent work, kind words, and a helping hand.
Coopertition
Competing and cooperating at the same time.
Looks like: different teams competing and still helping.
What to focus on first

Five priorities for Year 1

  • Learn the field and understand several missions before trying to solve everything.
  • Build a robot that starts the same way and moves predictably.
  • Make every child build, code, research, test, and explain at some point.
  • Start the Innovation Project early and talk to a real expert.
  • Use the Engineering Notebook to remember decisions, failures, feedback, and improvements.

Our Team Agreement for Kids, Parents & Coaches

💡⚙️
1 Our Goal 🎯

✓ Learn, build, solve problems, compete as a team, and have fun!

2 Our 6 Team Rules 🛡️
  1. Be kind and respectful. Listen when others speak. No teasing, blaming, yelling, or putting others down.
  2. Everyone participates. Everyone gets a chance to build, code, research, test, and present.
  3. Kids do the work. Coaches and parents guide, but the team makes the decisions and does the work.
  4. Challenge ideas, not people. It is okay to test a different idea. It is not okay to insult someone.
  5. Mistakes help us learn. If something fails, we test, improve, and try again.
  6. Have fun and support the team. We help each other and celebrate each other.
3 Role Rotation ⚙️

No permanent coder, builder, or presenter.
Roles rotate every 1 to 2 meetings.

  • Build
  • Code
  • Test the robot
  • Design attachments
  • Research the Innovation Project
  • Document our work
  • Present to judges
  • Operate the robot during practice

✓ Our Rule: Help your teammate learn. Don’t take over their job.

4 Working Together 🧑‍🤝‍🧑
  • Be ready to start on time.
  • One person speaks at a time.
  • Ask before changing someone else’s build or code.
  • Share robot, computer, and LEGO time fairly.
  • Stay focused during work time.
  • Clean up before leaving.

❓ Ask 2 teammates before asking the coach.

5 Disagreements 💬
  1. Listen to both ideas.
  2. Build or test when possible.
  3. Let the results help us decide.
  4. If needed, the team votes or the coach helps facilitate.
6 End of Every Meeting 📋
  1. What did we accomplish?
  2. What didn’t work?
  3. What do we do next?
  4. What are the next steps?
  5. Who owns what before the next meeting?
  6. Clean up before leaving.
7 At Competitions 🏆
  • Respect judges, referees, volunteers, and other teams.
  • No trash talking or blaming.
  • Cheer for our team and other teams.
  • Cheer for others, too.
  • Be honest about our work.
  • How we behave is just as important as our score.
8 Parent Guidelines 👨‍👩‍👧
  • Help kids arrive on time.
  • Let the coach know if your child will miss a meeting.
  • Encourage kids without solving problems for them.
  • Let kids explain their own robot, code, and project.
  • Keep competition and scores in perspective.
  • Share concerns privately with the coach.
9 Safety & Respect ❤️

Everyone should feel safe and welcome. No bullying, harassment, inappropriate language, physical aggression, or intentionally damaging another person’s work. If something feels uncomfortable, tell a coach or parent right away.

10 Our Team Promise ⭐

We will learn together. We will listen to each other. We will help each other. We will respect each other. We will keep trying. And we will have fun!

Common first-year traps

  • One child becomes the permanent coder or builder.
  • Adults fix the robot instead of asking kids guiding questions.
  • The team chases difficult points before reliable points.
  • The Innovation Project starts too late.
  • Kids memorize a presentation but cannot explain why decisions were made.
  • The team forgets to check official Challenge Updates.

How to choose missions for a first-year team

  1. Look for a clear scoring condition the kids understand.
  2. Prefer short travel distance and simple attachment movement.
  3. Test one mission at least 5 to 10 times.
  4. Record how many times it works without help.
  5. Only combine missions after each individual action is repeatable.
  6. Add high-risk bonuses later.

Simple scoring wording does not always mean an easy robot route. Test on the actual field.

A practical Robot Game progression

Stage 1Get 2 to 3 missions working separately.
Stage 2Make every launch position repeatable.
Stage 3Combine nearby missions into short runs.
Stage 4Practice recovery when one run fails.
Stage 5Run the complete 2.5-minute match again and again.

Robot Design: Learn the Engineering Story

Judges do not only want to see a finished robot. They want the kids to explain how the team identified challenges, designed ideas, created solutions, tested them, improved them, and communicated what it learned.

🧱 Build

Rigid driving base, aligned wheels, secured wires, accessible ports, and attachments that are easy to change.

💻 Code

Reliable distance, turns, motor control, sensor use, reusable blocks, and readable program names.

🧪 Test

Repeat the same run, measure success, change one thing at a time, and keep evidence of improvements.

🗣️ Explain

More than one child should be able to explain strategy, attachments, code, failures, and design choices.

Engineering Design Process

IdentifyDesignCreateIterateCommunicate

Robot skills worth learning early

  • Move a known distance forward and backward.
  • Turn left and right consistently.
  • Use a wall or jig to align the robot before launch.
  • Run a motor attachment a controlled amount.
  • Use functions or custom blocks for repeated code.
  • Understand when a sensor can improve reliability.
  • Reset and recover quickly after a failed run.

Questions judges may ask

  • How did you decide which missions to solve?
  • What part of the robot changed the most?
  • What test failed, and what did you learn?
  • How did you make your programs more reliable?
  • What attachment are you most proud of?
  • How did team members share coding and building?

Robot Design reference: what “ready” looks like

  • ✓The kids can explain the robot without the coach speaking for them.
  • ✓The team has examples of a failure, a change, and a better result.
  • ✓Multiple kids understand the code and attachments.
  • ✓The team can explain why it chose its mission strategy.
  • ✓The Engineering Notebook contains useful evidence, not just final answers.

Innovation Lab: From Biodiversity Problem to Prototype

The best 4th-grade projects usually start with a problem kids can observe or understand locally. The prototype does not need to be expensive, electronic, or perfect. It needs to help the team test and improve its idea.

1. IdentifyChoose one clear biodiversity problem.
2. ResearchLearn what already exists and who is affected.
3. DesignSketch several solutions before choosing.
4. CreateBuild something others can see or test.
5. IterateGet feedback and make meaningful changes.
6. CommunicateExplain problem, evidence, solution, feedback, and impact.
Great 4th-grade project test: Can the kids explain the problem in one sentence, build a simple prototype in one or two meetings, test one important idea, and find a real person who can give useful feedback?

Project Idea Library

These are starting points. The team should research the problem and create its own solution.

Strong first-year choice

🐦 Bird-Safe Windows

Problem: Birds can collide with reflective or transparent glass.

Kid question: How could a school or home window become easier for birds to see without making the room dark?

Prototype: Clear plastic sheets or picture frames with several visible pattern designs. Test visibility from different distances and angles, then improve the pattern.

Ask: Mass Audubon, a bird biologist, building manager, or local conservation group.

Strong first-year choice

🌼 Pollinator Pathway

Problem: Pollinators need connected food and habitat through the growing season.

Kid question: Could small native-plant patches make a schoolyard or neighborhood more useful for pollinators?

Prototype: A scaled cardboard or LEGO site model with several planting layouts, plus a seasonal “bloom calendar” showing when food is available.

Ask: Native Plant Trust, Arnold Arboretum, Mass Audubon, a local garden club, or a plant ecologist.

Strong first-year choice

🗺️ Schoolyard Biodiversity Map

Problem: People often do not know which parts of a schoolyard support the most living things.

Kid question: Can a simple observation system help a school protect or improve biodiversity hotspots?

Prototype: A paper or digital map with observation zones, species cards, habitat scores, and a mock dashboard that suggests improvements.

Ask: A biology teacher, city conservation staff, MassWildlife, SciStarter, or a university ecologist.

Strong first-year choice

💡 Wildlife-Friendly Night Lighting

Problem: Artificial light can change the behavior of insects and other wildlife.

Kid question: Can lighting be directed where people need it while reducing unnecessary light into habitat?

Prototype: A shoebox street or park with small LEDs and interchangeable shields. Compare how much light spills into a model habitat.

Ask: An ecologist, town sustainability staff, a lighting engineer, or Mass Audubon.

Strong first-year choice

🌿 Invasive Plant Early Warning

Problem: Invasive plants can spread before people notice them.

Kid question: How could kids or families spot and report a few priority invasive plants more easily?

Prototype: Simple field ID cards, a color-coded map, QR-code concept, or mock app screens. Test whether another student can identify examples correctly.

Ask: Native Plant Trust, a town conservation commission, or a plant ecologist.

🌧️ Rain Garden for Habitat

Problem: Fast runoff can carry pollution and leave little habitat value.

Prototype: Two tray landscapes, one conventional and one rain-garden design. Pour equal water and compare runoff while showing where native plants could live.

🌉 Mini Wildlife Corridor

Problem: Roads, fences, and development can break habitat into disconnected pieces.

Prototype: LEGO or cardboard landscape with several corridor designs. Use tokens or a simple rule-based simulation to compare connectivity.

🗑️ Stream Litter Interceptor

Problem: Trash can enter streams while wildlife still needs to move through the water.

Prototype: A tabletop water channel with several removable barrier designs. Use safe floating objects to compare litter capture and open passage.

🌳 Tree Health Helper

Problem: Young urban trees face heat, dry soil, compaction, and damage.

Prototype: A model tree pit with different mulch, shade, or watering concepts, plus a simple observation guide for signs of stress.

📷 Wildlife Observation Station

Problem: People need better information about which animals use an area and when.

Prototype: A mock camera-trap housing, observation protocol, species log, and dashboard concept. No animals need to be captured or disturbed.

🍂 Soil Life Protection

Problem: Removing all leaves and dead plant material can reduce shelter and food for small organisms.

Prototype: A schoolyard “leave the leaves” zone plan, signage, and miniature habitat layers that explain where organisms live.

🌾 Native Seed Recovery

Problem: Habitat restoration needs the right native plants in the right places.

Prototype: A seed-sorting or planting-planner concept that matches mock seed types to sun, moisture, and habitat conditions.

What Can a 4th-Grade Prototype Look Like?

📦 Physical Model

Cardboard, LEGO, craft materials, 3D print, tubes, paper, or recycled materials that demonstrate how a solution works.

🧪 Test Rig

A simple experiment that compares two or three versions and produces evidence, such as runoff, light spill, visibility, or capture rate.

📱 App Mockup

Paper screens or a clickable slide deck showing what an app would do. Coding the full app is not required to test the idea.

🗺️ System or Map

A model showing where a solution goes, how people use it, and how wildlife or habitat benefits.

🧰 Toolkit

Identification cards, instructions, labels, measurement tools, or a classroom kit that helps people take action.

🎭 Demonstration

A short role-play or model that shows the problem before the solution and after the solution.

The 4th-Grade Prototype Recipe

  1. Write the problem: “We noticed that…”
  2. Name the user: Who would use or benefit from the solution?
  3. Sketch three ideas: Do not fall in love with the first idea.
  4. Build Version 1: Use simple materials and make the main idea visible.
  5. Choose one test: What important question can the team measure or observe?
  6. Get feedback: Ask an expert and at least one possible user.
  7. Build Version 2: Change something because of the feedback or test.
  8. Save the story: Photograph both versions and explain what improved.

Innovation Project mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing a huge topic such as “save all endangered animals.”
  • Building a gadget before understanding the real problem.
  • Calling something “new” without researching existing solutions.
  • Making a prototype but never testing it.
  • Talking to an expert only after the project is finished.
  • Letting adults design the solution.

Seven great expert questions

  1. Is this a real and important problem?
  2. Who or what is most affected?
  3. What solutions already exist?
  4. What is the biggest weakness in our idea?
  5. What should we measure or test?
  6. What one change would make our prototype more useful?
  7. Who else should we talk to?

12-Week First-Year Roadmap

Adjust the pace to your event date. The goal is to avoid leaving the Innovation Project or judging preparation until the end.

Weeks 1-2

Explore and Build Foundations

Ways of Working, BIOGLOW theme, mission models, field setup, basic driving, Project Sparks, and role rotation.

Weeks 3-4

Learn Core Robot Skills

Reliable movement, turns, simple attachment, test early missions, shortlist 3 Innovation Project problems.

Weeks 5-6

Choose and Research

Get several missions repeatable, choose one project problem, research existing solutions, and contact experts.

Weeks 7-8

Create and Combine

Combine robot missions into short routes, build Innovation Prototype V1, get feedback, and create V2.

Weeks 9-10

Practice the Whole Story

Full robot matches, project explanation, Robot Design evidence, Core Values examples, and improvements based on weak spots.

Week 11

Mock Event

Timed matches, mock judging questions, presentation practice, role transitions, and fixing only the biggest reliability issues.

Week 12

Competition Ready

Final practice, pack equipment, confirm official updates, protect sleep and energy, celebrate the work, and have fun.

By the middle of the season

  • ✓Several robot missions work reliably.
  • ✓The project problem is specific.
  • ✓The team has contacted at least one expert.
  • ✓Every child has rotated through multiple roles.

Two weeks before competition

  • ✓The team can run a full Robot Game match.
  • ✓Prototype improvements are documented.
  • ✓Kids can explain Robot Design without adult help.
  • ✓The team has practiced real judging questions.
